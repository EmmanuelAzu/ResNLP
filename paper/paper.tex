\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
Isigidimi sama-Xosa was founded by missionaries; Imvo Zabantsundu (1884) was the first African-owned isiXhosa paper. Did that difference leave a trace in what each paper covered (salience) or in how it used words (framing)? Against frequency-matched random words, salience separates the papers: Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary about 3.3 times as often and Isigidimi religious vocabulary about four times as often. Neither survives correction for 15 tests, so the case rests on effect size, breadth and the archive's own account. Framing is weaker and less specific: embedding spaces differ globally, but no concept is robustly shifted, and a parliament-and-vote signal at the tuned window fades at a wider one. Historical text costs modern tokenizers 2--12\% more subwords per character; spelling alone raises the cost only for the African-language model (about 2\% per word).}

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
The IsiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023} holds South Africa's earliest African-language press. \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} (1870--88) was founded by missionaries; \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} was the first African-owned paper, free ``from any missionary control'' \citep[p.~55]{schoots2023}. The archive's authors describe Isigidimi's missionary foundation as ``revealed in the frequency of church and Bible stories'' \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}; earlier studies trace single concepts such as \emph{imfundo} \citep{dewet2021,sigenu2021,qazisa2022}. We add a controlled computational complement built on communication research's distinction between \emph{agenda-setting}, what is covered \citep{mccombs1972}, and \emph{framing}, how it is presented \citep{entman1993}, operationalised as \textbf{salience} (the rate of a concept's vocabulary) and \textbf{framing} (the company a word keeps in embedding space; \citealp{hamilton2016}). We also ask whether modern tokenizers penalise historical text \citep{petrov2023,rust2021}. Hypotheses: \textbf{H1} the papers differ in the salience of political and religious vocabulary; \textbf{H2} they differ in the framing of political concepts, Isigidimi's lying closer to religious contexts; \textbf{H3} historical text, and its spelling, raises tokenizer cost.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use the isiXhosa blocks of the archive's human-corrected transcriptions (no spell-checking, so spelling and word division are the concern; \citealp[p.~59]{schoots2023}). The window runs from 1 November 1884 (Imvo's first issue) to 1888; Isigidimi's 1886 editions are missing from the archive \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}. Isigidimi has 286{,}663 tokens, Imvo 1.31M; for each year-month with Isigidimi text we draw Imvo pages from that month to match its token count, ten times.

\textbf{Salience.} Concepts are stems (Table S1): 10 political concepts fixed before testing, four neutral controls, and exploratory religion \emph{tixo}/\emph{kristu}, suggested by an issue-grouped nested-cross-validated classifier (0.83 balanced accuracy). Rate ratios per million tokens carry issue-bootstrap CIs and are ranked against 2000 pseudo-concepts (random word bags of the same combined frequency); the 15 tests are uncorrected. Isigidimi 1876--83 vs Imvo 1889--94 changes paper and era together, so it tests era sensitivity, calibrated by within-paper drift.

\textbf{Framing.} We use fastText skip-gram \citep{bojanowski2017}, more stable than word2vec here (0.83 vs 0.64). Hyper-parameters were tuned on out-of-window text by seed stability \citep{antoniak2018} until dimension and epochs plateaued (dim 400, window 1, the smallest, 20 epochs; 30 scored 0.002 lower). A window-5 run checks sensitivity to window size. A word's \emph{profile shift} is $1-r$ between its cosine profiles over the 500 most frequent shared words in the two spaces \citep{hamilton2016}. The null pools issues from both papers and reassigns them to two token-balanced groups (50 permutations); as real groups differ in frequency and style but permuted ones do not, each concept is also ranked against 2000 frequency-matched non-concept word sets. A directional test compares similarity to religious and political anchors (Table S2). A control joins disjunctively written forms (\emph{uku ba}$\to$\emph{ukuba}).

\textbf{Tokenizers.} On modern isiXhosa news \citep{adelani2023} we measure subwords for XLM-R \citep{conneau2020}, Serengeti \citep{adebara2023} and mBERT in (a) modern text, (b) the same text with aspiration marks stripped (\emph{th}$\to$\emph{t} etc.) and (c) historical text. Spelling alone (a to b) is judged per word, since the words are identical; historical text (a to c) per character, since word division differs. All runs are CPU-only (about three hours).

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Salience (H1).} Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary 3.3$\times$ as often (CI 2.6--4.6; pseudo-concept $p=0.011$) and Isigidimi religious vocabulary four times as often (0.24$\times$, $p=0.019$); neither survives correction for 15 tests ($p<0.003$) and nothing else lies beyond the matched null (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, left). The held-out windows change era as well as paper, so they test era sensitivity, not independence: within-paper drift is 0.6--1.8$\times$ for parliament and religion, far below the between-paper gaps, but 0.4--0.65$\times$ for government, whose flip (0.93$\to$0.27) signals a stem or era problem and is not interpreted (Table S3).

\textbf{Framing (H2).} Mean profile shift between the papers is 0.348 against a permutation null of 0.230, so concepts are judged against matched word sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, right). Parliament alone reaches the 97th percentile ($p=0.035$), as high as a neutral control (cattle, 90th--99th): framing and salience stand on the same uncorrected footing. Parliament and vote pooled reach the 92nd--99th percentile at window 1 ($p=0.010$--0.083; Table S2), but at window 5 parliament alone falls to the 47th ($p=0.529$) and the pooled profile shift to the 62nd (Jaccard: 98th). Joining disjunctively written forms leaves the global gap unchanged (0.118, 0.118). The directional lean towards religious anchors is positive in every condition but significant in one of five ($p=0.042$, uncorrected) and none after correction. The detection floor: a one-word concept must shift by 0.15 to register, more than the whole paper-level gap (0.118), an 8-form concept by 0.05.

\textbf{Tokenizers (H3).} Per word, stripping aspiration raises Serengeti's cost by 2.2\% but lowers XLM-R's and mBERT's ($-$2.2\%, $-$3.4\%); per character, historical text costs 12.4\% more for Serengeti, 4.1\% for XLM-R and 1.7\% for mBERT (CIs within $\pm0.5$\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:two2}, right).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Agenda more than framing.} As uncorrected $p$-values are similar on both sides, we favour agenda on other grounds: effect size (3.3$\times$ and four-fold, against framing results borderline at window 1 and absent at window 5), breadth (two concepts show large salience effects against one fragile framing result; religion cannot be tested for framing because its words seed the anchors) and corroboration (the archive's account of church and Bible stories; \citealp[p.~57]{schoots2023}). A selection mechanism would explain this: both papers carried correspondents' reports and readers' letters, so an editor's chief power was choosing what to print, which moves salience (agenda-setting; \citealp{mccombs1972}) without changing how contributors used words (framing; \citealp{entman1993}). We can rule out framing shifts above the detection floor, not smaller ones; the 1885 type labels (editorials vs letters), if exportable, could test the mechanism.

\textbf{Attribution.} Parliamentary vocabulary enters Isigidimi in 1883--84, when Jabavu reportedly edited it (secondary sources, unconfirmed) and publication was fortnightly before an election; an editor effect is not identified.

\textbf{Why framing is hard to see.} Words shift globally but rarely specifically; the most-shifted are function words, verb forms and \emph{xosa} (Isigidimi's spelling). Joining forms left the gap unchanged, so word division is not the explanation. A wider window removed parliament's shift rather than strengthening it, so the null is not a tuning artefact and the window-1 signal is not robust.

\textbf{Responsible NLP.} Tokenizer costs fall hardest on the African-language model (pretraining data unlisted, overlap unverified). The archive's authors plan to explore machine-learning labelling and named-entity recognition; our result predicts (untested downstream) that such tools will serve 1880s isiXhosa worst, at its speakers' cost. The archive preserves a small literate, mission-educated elite filtered by editors: shifts describe these papers, not isiXhosa speakers or a colonised community, and reading them as cultural claims without speaker review would be extractive. Stems, glosses and neighbours await that review; \emph{rulumente} is glossed ``parliament'' in the archive paper \citep[p.~62]{schoots2023}, ``government'' here. Limitations: two papers, one window, a missing year, era-confounded held-out windows, no editor metadata, a domain mismatch.
