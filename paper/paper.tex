\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
Isigidimi sama-Xosa was founded by missionaries; Imvo Zabantsundu (1884) was the first African-owned isiXhosa paper. Did that leave a trace in what each paper covered (salience) or how it used words (framing)? Against frequency-matched random words, salience separates the papers: Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary about 3.3 times as often and Isigidimi religious vocabulary about four times as often. Neither survives correction for 15 tests, but both exceed within-paper era drift and match the archive's own account. Framing evidence is weaker and less specific: embedding spaces differ globally and no single concept is robustly shifted, but political concepts lean towards religious contexts in Isigidimi in all five settings tried, a lean that is partly concept-specific and matched by a neutral control: hypothesis-generating. Historical text costs modern tokenizers 2--12\% more subwords per character; spelling alone raises the cost only for the African-language model (about 2\% per word).}

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
The IsiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023} holds South Africa's earliest African-language press. \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} (1870--88) was founded by missionaries; \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} was the first African-owned paper, free ``from any missionary control'' \citep[p.~55]{schoots2023}. The archive's authors describe Isigidimi's missionary foundation as ``revealed in the frequency of church and Bible stories'' \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}; earlier studies trace single concepts such as \emph{imfundo} \citep{dewet2021,sigenu2021,qazisa2022}. We add a controlled computational complement built on communication research's distinction between \emph{agenda-setting}, what is covered \citep{mccombs1972}, and \emph{framing}, how it is presented \citep{entman1993}, operationalised as \textbf{salience} (the rate of a concept's vocabulary) and \textbf{framing} (the company a word keeps in embedding space; \citealp{hamilton2016}). We also ask whether modern tokenizers penalise historical text \citep{petrov2023,rust2021}. Hypotheses: \textbf{H1} the papers differ in the salience of political and religious vocabulary; \textbf{H2} they differ in the framing of political concepts, Isigidimi's lying closer to religious contexts; \textbf{H3} historical text, and its spelling, raises tokenizer cost.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use the isiXhosa blocks of the archive's human-corrected transcriptions (no spell-checking, so spelling and word division are the concern; \citealp[p.~59]{schoots2023}). The window runs from 1 November 1884 (Imvo's first issue) to 1888; Isigidimi's 1886 editions are missing from the archive \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}. Isigidimi has 286{,}663 tokens, Imvo 1.31M; for each year-month with Isigidimi text we draw Imvo pages from that month to match its token count, ten times.

\textbf{Salience.} Concepts are stems (Table S1): 10 political concepts fixed before testing, four neutral controls, and exploratory religion \emph{tixo}/\emph{kristu}, suggested by an issue-grouped nested-cross-validated classifier (0.83 balanced accuracy). Rate ratios per million tokens carry issue-bootstrap CIs and are ranked against 2000 pseudo-concepts (random word bags of the same combined frequency); the 15 tests are uncorrected. Isigidimi 1876--83 vs Imvo 1889--94 changes paper and era together, so it tests era sensitivity, calibrated by within-paper drift.

\textbf{Framing.} We use fastText skip-gram \citep{bojanowski2017}, more stable than word2vec here (0.83 vs 0.64). Hyper-parameters were tuned on out-of-window text by seed stability \citep{antoniak2018} until dimension and epochs plateaued (dim 400, window 1, the smallest, 20 epochs; 30 scored 0.002 lower). Window 5 checks sensitivity. A word's \emph{profile shift} is $1-r$ between its cosine profiles over the 500 most frequent shared words in the two spaces \citep{hamilton2016}. The null pools issues from both papers and reassigns them to two token-balanced groups (50 permutations); as real groups differ in frequency and style but permuted ones do not, each concept is also ranked against 2000 frequency-matched non-concept word sets. A directional test compares similarity to religious and political anchors. A control joins disjunctively written forms (\emph{uku ba}$\to$\emph{ukuba}).

\textbf{Tokenizers.} On modern isiXhosa news \citep{adelani2023} we measure subwords for XLM-R \citep{conneau2020}, Serengeti \citep{adebara2023} and mBERT in (a) modern text, (b) the same text with aspiration marks stripped (\emph{th}$\to$\emph{t} etc.) and (c) historical text. Spelling alone (a to b) is judged per word, since the words are identical; historical text (a to c) per character, since word division differs. All runs are CPU-only (about three hours).

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Salience (H1).} Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary 3.3$\times$ as often (CI 2.6--4.6; pseudo-concept $p=0.018$) and Isigidimi religious vocabulary four times as often (0.24$\times$, $p=0.020$); neither survives correction for 15 tests ($p<0.003$) and nothing else lies beyond the matched null (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, left). The held-out windows change era as well as paper, so they test era sensitivity, not independence: within-paper drift is 0.6--1.8$\times$ for parliament and religion, far below the between-paper gaps, but 0.4--0.65$\times$ for government, whose flip (0.93$\to$0.27) is not interpreted (Table S3).

\textbf{Framing (H2).} Mean profile shift between the papers is 0.348 against a permutation null of 0.230, so concepts are judged against matched word sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, right). No single concept is robust: parliament alone reaches the 96th percentile ($p=0.045$), as high as a neutral control (cattle, 90th--99th). Parliament and vote pooled shift on the neighbour-overlap (Jaccard) measure in all five settings (94th--98th percentile, $p=0.016$--0.061) but on the profile measure only at narrow windows (86th--99th at windows 1--2, 62nd at window 5; Table S2). Joining word forms leaves the global gap unchanged (0.118, 0.118). Directionally, political concepts lean towards religious anchors in Isigidimi in all five settings (pooled $\Delta$ +0.44 to +0.76, $p<0.016$; neutral controls pooled $|\Delta|\leq0.38$, $p\geq0.16$);  but without \emph{land} (\emph{umhlaba}) $p$ is 0.010--0.256, and one control (\emph{house}) shifts as much ($+0.8$ to $+1.5$). The detection floor: a one-word concept must shift by 0.15 to register, more than the whole paper-level gap (0.118), a 4-form set by 0.08.

\textbf{Tokenizers (H3).} Per word, stripping aspiration raises Serengeti's cost by 2.2\% but lowers XLM-R's and mBERT's ($-$2.2\%, $-$3.4\%); per character, historical text costs 12.4\% more for Serengeti, 4.1\% for XLM-R and 1.7\% for mBERT (CIs within $\pm0.5$\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:two2}, right).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Agenda more than framing, not agenda only.} Salience effects are large, exceed within-paper era drift and match the archive's account of church and Bible stories \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}; framing evidence is weaker: no single concept is robust, and the directional lean is exploratory, partly rests on \emph{land} and is matched by a neutral control. Uncorrected $p$-values are similar on both sides, so the ranking rests on robustness and corroboration. A selection mechanism would explain the salience gap: both papers carried correspondents' reports and readers' letters, so an editor's chief power was choosing what to print (agenda-setting; \citealp{mccombs1972}); a real directional lean would mean contributors also wrote about politics in religious terms (framing; \citealp{entman1993}). We can rule out framing shifts above the detection floor, not smaller ones; the 1885 type labels, if exportable, could test the mechanism.

\textbf{Attribution.} Parliamentary vocabulary enters Isigidimi in 1883--84, when it appeared fortnightly \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}; an editor effect cannot be separated from publication frequency or events (editor metadata would settle it).

\textbf{Why framing is hard to see.} Words shift globally but rarely specifically; the most-shifted are function words, verb forms and \emph{xosa} (Isigidimi's spelling). Joining forms left the gap unchanged, so word division is not the explanation. At window 5 the pooled profile shift fades but neighbour overlap and the directional lean persist: the evidence depends on the measure.

\textbf{Responsible NLP.} Tokenizer costs fall hardest on the African-language model (pretraining data unlisted, overlap unverified). The archive's authors plan to explore machine-learning labelling and named-entity recognition; our result predicts (untested downstream) that such tools will serve 1880s isiXhosa worst. The archive preserves a small literate, mission-educated elite filtered by editors: shifts describe these papers, not isiXhosa speakers or a colonised community, and reading them as cultural claims without speaker review would be extractive. Stems and glosses await that review (our first \emph{law} stem also matched \emph{intetho}, `speech', and was dropped); \emph{rulumente} is glossed ``parliament'' in the archive paper \citep[p.~62]{schoots2023}, ``government'' here. Limitations: two papers, one window, a missing year, era-confounded held-out windows, no editor metadata, a domain mismatch.
