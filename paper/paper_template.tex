\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
Where did missionary control leave its trace in the language of the earliest isiXhosa press: in what the papers covered (salience) or in how they used words (framing)? We compare the missionary \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} with \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded free of missionary control, against frequency-matched random word sets. Salience separates the papers: Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary about 3.3 times as often and Isigidimi religious vocabulary about four times as often. Neither survives correction for 15 tests, but both exceed within-paper era drift and match the archive's own account. Framing evidence is weaker and less specific: embedding spaces differ globally and no single concept is robustly shifted, but political concepts lean towards religious contexts in Isigidimi in all five settings tried, a lean that is partly concept-specific and matched by a neutral control: hypothesis-generating. Historical text costs modern tokenizers 2--12\% more subwords per character; spelling alone raises the cost only for the African-language model (about 2\% per word). The papers differ clearly in what they printed; whether missionary control changed how political language was used lies below what about 300k tokens can reliably detect.}

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Where did missionary control leave its trace in the language of the earliest isiXhosa press?} The IsiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023} holds South Africa's earliest African-language newspapers. \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} (1870--88) was a missionary paper; \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded in 1884 by J.\,T.~Jabavu, was the first African-owned paper, free ``from any missionary control'' \citep[p.~55]{schoots2023}. The archive's authors see Isigidimi's origin ``revealed in the frequency of church and Bible stories'' \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}; earlier studies trace single concepts such as \emph{imfundo} (`education'; \citealp{dewet2021,sigenu2021,qazisa2022}). We add a controlled computational test, using communication research's distinction between \emph{agenda-setting}, what is covered \citep{mccombs1972}, and \emph{framing}, how it is presented \citep{entman1993}. We measure the first as \textbf{salience} (the rate of a concept's vocabulary) and the second as the company a word keeps in embedding space \citep{hamilton2016}. We also ask whether modern tokenizers, which such text must pass through, penalise it \citep{petrov2023,rust2021}. \textbf{H1} The papers differ in the salience of political and religious vocabulary. \textbf{H2} They differ in the framing of political concepts, Isigidimi's lying closer to religious contexts. \textbf{H3} Historical text, and its spelling, raises tokenizer cost.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use the isiXhosa blocks of the archive's human-corrected transcriptions (no spell-checking, so spelling and word division are the concern; \citealp[p.~59]{schoots2023}). The window runs from 1 November 1884 (Imvo's first issue) to 1888; Isigidimi's 1886 editions are missing from the archive \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}. Isigidimi has 286{,}663 tokens, Imvo 1.31M; for each year-month with Isigidimi text we draw Imvo pages from that month to match its token count, ten times.

\textbf{Salience.} Concepts are stems (Table S1): 10 political concepts fixed before testing, four neutral controls, and exploratory religion \emph{tixo}/\emph{kristu}, suggested by an issue-grouped nested-cross-validated classifier (@@CLFACC@@ balanced accuracy). Rate ratios per million tokens carry issue-bootstrap CIs and are ranked against 2000 matched sets (random word types of the same combined frequency); the 15 tests are uncorrected. Isigidimi 1876--83 vs Imvo 1889--94 changes paper and era together, so it tests era sensitivity, calibrated by within-paper drift.

\textbf{Framing.} We use fastText skip-gram \citep{bojanowski2017}, more stable than word2vec here (@@FTSTAB@@ vs @@W2VSTAB@@). Hyper-parameters were tuned on out-of-window text by seed stability \citep{antoniak2018} until dimension and epochs plateaued (dim 400, window 1, the smallest, 20 epochs; 30 scored 0.002 lower). Window 5 checks sensitivity. A word's \emph{profile shift} is $1-r$ between its cosine profiles over the 500 most frequent shared words in the two spaces \citep{hamilton2016}. The null pools issues from both papers and reassigns them to two token-balanced groups (50 permutations); as real groups differ in frequency and style but permuted ones do not, each concept is also ranked against 2000 matched sets. A directional test compares similarity to religious and political anchors. A control joins disjunctively written forms (\emph{uku ba}$\to$\emph{ukuba}).

\textbf{Tokenizers.} On modern isiXhosa news \citep{adelani2023} we measure subwords for XLM-R \citep{conneau2020}, Serengeti \citep{adebara2023} and mBERT in (a) modern text, (b) the same text with aspiration marks stripped (\emph{th}$\to$\emph{t} etc.) and (c) historical text. Spelling alone (a to b) is judged per word, since the words are identical; historical text (a to c) per character, since word division differs.

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Salience (H1).} Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary @@SPAR@@$\times$ as often (CI @@SPARLO@@--@@SPARHI@@; matched-set $p=@@SPARP@@$) and Isigidimi religious vocabulary four times as often (@@SREL@@$\times$, $p=@@SRELP@@$); neither survives correction for 15 tests ($p<0.003$) and nothing else lies beyond the matched sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, left). Within-paper era drift is only @@ERAPR@@$\times$ for both, far below the gaps; government drifts @@GOVDRIFT@@$\times$ and is not interpreted (Table S3).

\textbf{Framing (H2).} Mean profile shift between the papers is @@OBS@@ against a permutation null of @@NULL@@, so concepts are judged against matched word sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, right). No single concept is robust: parliament alone reaches the @@PARU@@ percentile ($p=@@PARUP@@$), as high as a neutral control (cattle, @@CATLO@@--@@CATHI@@). Parliament and vote pooled shift on the neighbour-overlap (Jaccard) measure in all five settings (@@JPVLO@@--@@JPVHI@@ percentile, $p=@@JPVPLO@@$--@@JPVPHI@@) but on the profile measure only at narrow windows (@@PROFLO@@--@@PROFHI@@ at windows 1--2, @@W5POOLP@@ at window 5; Table S2). The detection floor for a one-word concept (@@FLOOR1@@) exceeds the whole paper-level gap (@@GAP1@@).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Agenda more than framing (H1, H2).} Salience effects are large, exceed within-paper era drift and match the archive's account of church and Bible stories \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}; framing evidence is weaker: no single concept is robust, and the directional lean is exploratory, partly rests on \emph{land} and is matched by a neutral control. The ranking rests on robustness and corroboration, not $p$-values. Selection would explain the salience gap: editors chose which correspondents' reports and letters to print (agenda-setting); a real directional lean would mean contributors also wrote about politics in religious terms (framing). Framing shifts above the detection floor are ruled out, smaller ones are not.

\textbf{Attribution (H1).} Parliamentary vocabulary enters Isigidimi in 1883--84, when it appeared fortnightly \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}; an editor effect cannot be separated from publication frequency or events.

\textbf{Why framing is hard to see (H2).} Words shift globally but rarely specifically; the most-shifted are function words, verb forms and \emph{xosa} (Isigidimi's spelling). Joining disjunctively written forms left the gap unchanged (@@GAP1@@, @@GAP2@@). At window 5 the pooled profile shift fades but neighbour overlap and the directional lean persist: the evidence depends on the measure.

\textbf{Responsible NLP (H3).} Tokenizer costs fall hardest on the African-language model. Tools the archive's authors plan (labelling, named-entity recognition) may thus serve 1880s isiXhosa worst (untested). The archive preserves a small literate, mission-educated elite filtered by editors: shifts describe these papers, not isiXhosa speakers or a colonised community, and reading them as cultural claims without speaker review would be extractive. Stems and glosses await that review (our first \emph{law} stem also matched \emph{intetho}, `speech', and was dropped); \emph{rulumente} (`government') is glossed ``parliament'' in the archive paper \citep[p.~62]{schoots2023}, ``government'' here. Limitations: two papers, one window, a missing year, era-confounded held-out windows, no editor metadata. The papers differ clearly in what they printed; whether missionary control changed how political language was used lies below what about 300k tokens can reliably detect.
