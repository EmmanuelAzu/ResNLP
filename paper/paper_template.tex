\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
From 1884 the missionary \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} and the African-owned \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} ran side by side. Did missionary control show in \emph{what} they printed or \emph{how} they used words? Imvo was not more political in vocabulary: of ten political concepts fixed in advance, only parliament differs (3.3$\times$, uncorrected). Exploratory religion differs most: Isigidimi uses it four times as often, in readers' letters too, and the gap holds both in the same years and under the same editor, Jabavu. Framing evidence is weak and not specific to political words. Old spelling penalises only the African-language tokenizer. The results are consistent with control shaping what the papers printed; whether it shaped how they wrote lies below our detection floor.}

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Where did missionary control leave its trace in the language of the earliest isiXhosa press?} In the IsiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023}, \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} was a missionary paper; \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded in 1884 by Isigidimi's former editor J.\,T.~Jabavu, was the first African-owned isiXhosa paper, free ``from any missionary control'' \citep[p.~55]{schoots2023}. From late 1884 both had African editors, Gqoba and Jabavu \citep[p.~71]{schoots2023b}. Comparing the papers contrasts control, but also two editors, places and audiences, which one paper per side cannot separate. So we also hold the editor constant: Jabavu edited Isigidimi in 1881--84, then Imvo.

Communication research separates \emph{agenda-setting}, what a paper covers \citep{mccombs1972}, from \emph{framing}, how it presents it \citep{entman1993}. We measure the first as \textbf{salience}, how often a topic's words appear, and the second with word embeddings \citep{hamilton2016}. Such studies mostly use modern news \citep{card2015,field2018}; \citet{schoots2023b} counted root words in these papers. We add controls that make both testable, the same-editor contrast, and a letters-versus-rest split to ask \emph{how} control worked; finally, we ask whether this history still shapes how machines read the archive \citep{petrov2023,rust2021}. \textbf{H1}: the papers differ in how often they use political and religious words. \textbf{H2}: Isigidimi's political words sit closer to religious contexts. \textbf{H3}: historical text and its spelling raise tokenizer cost.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use the archive's human-corrected transcriptions, not spell-checked \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}, from November 1884 to 1888: 286{,}663 tokens of Isigidimi and 1.31M of Imvo.

\textbf{Salience.} Concepts are word stems adapted from \citet{schoots2023b} (Table S1): ten political concepts fixed in advance, four neutral controls, and religion (\emph{tixo}, \emph{kristu}). Religion is exploratory: the archive's authors expect it \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}, but a classifier trained on the same years chose it (logistic regression, issue-grouped nested cross-validation, @@CLFACC@@ balanced accuracy; chance 0.50). Ratios carry issue-bootstrap CIs; Jabavu's Isigidimi runs from January 1881 to October 1884.

\textbf{Letters.} A letter opens by addressing the editor (\emph{nkosi mhleli \dots\ kaundifakele}, `sir, editor \dots\ please print') and ends with a signature; @@LETPREC@@ random detections were genuine letters (Table S5).

\textbf{Framing.} Embedding shift is a weak proxy for Entman's framing: it also reflects sense, grammar and topic. We train fastText embeddings \citep{bojanowski2017}, tuning @@NTUNE@@ settings on other years for seed stability, since unstable embeddings fake shifts \citep{antoniak2018} (@@FTSTAB@@; word2vec @@W2VSTAB@@; Table S8). This favours a grammar-heavy window of 1, so we also report windows 2 and 5. A word's \emph{profile shift} is $1-r$ between its similarities to 500 common words in each paper \citep{hamilton2016b}; chance comes from shuffling issues \citep{dubossarsky2017}. The directional test, aimed at framing, asks whether political words sit nearer religious or political anchors (Table S4); to read it, we count religious and legal words near \emph{umhlaba}.

\textbf{Tokenizers.} We count subwords for XLM-R \citep{conneau2020}, Serengeti \citep{adebara2023} and mBERT \citep{devlin2019} on modern news \citep{adelani2023}, on the same news in old spelling (per word) and on our text (per character).



\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{H1: different things, but not more politics.} Of ten political concepts, only parliament differs: Imvo uses it @@SPAR@@ times as often ($p=@@SPARP@@$ against matched sets; Fig.~\ref{fig:agenda}); law, vote, rights, land and government do not. Exploratory religion does: Isigidimi uses it @@SRELINV@@ times as often ($p=@@SRELP@@$). Neither survives correction for 15 tests ($p<0.003$). (CIs ask if rates differ; matched sets, if a concept differs more than random words.) The religious gap holds in letters (@@LREL@@) as in the rest (@@RREL@@; Fig.~\ref{fig:agenda}, right). Under the same editor, Jabavu, Imvo carried religious words a third as often as Isigidimi (@@ECREL@@, CI @@ECRELLO@@--@@ECRELHI@@, matched-set $p=@@ECRELP@@$), and parliamentary words @@ECPAR@@ times as often ($p=@@ECPARP@@$). Under Gqoba, Isigidimi carried religious words @@EDREL@@ times as often as under Jabavu ($p=@@EDRELP@@$). Within Isigidimi, religious words had already fallen to @@ETREL@@ of their 1876--80 level by 1881--84, while parliament rose @@ETPAR@@-fold and vote @@ETVOTE@@-fold ($p\leq@@ETPOLP@@$; Fig.~\ref{fig:how}a; Table S7).

\textbf{H2: framing differences are weak and not specific.} Word use differs overall (profile shift @@OBS@@ vs @@NULL@@ by chance); no concept stands out: parliament reaches the @@PARU@@ percentile of matched sets, the control \emph{cattle} @@CATLO@@--@@CATHI@@ (Fig.~S1). Political words lean towards religious anchors in Isigidimi in all five settings ($\Delta$ @@DIRLO@@ to @@DIRHI@@ $z$-units; Bonferroni-significant in @@NBON@@; Table S2). But without \emph{land} the lean is rarely significant ($p=@@NOLANDLO@@$--$@@NOLANDHI@@$); the control \emph{house} leans further (@@HOUSE2LO@@ to @@HOUSE2HI@@)@@ENDLUSENT@@; and \emph{umhlaba} sits near religious words in @@LSRI@@\% of Isigidimi's uses but @@LSRM@@\% of Imvo's (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}b). A one-word concept must shift by @@FLOOR1@@ (1.645 matched-set SDs) to be detected, more than the overall gap (@@GAP1@@).

\textbf{H3: historical text costs more.} Per character, our text needs @@SHI@@\%, @@XHI@@\% and @@MHI@@\% more subwords with Serengeti, XLM-R and mBERT; old spelling alone raises only Serengeti's (+@@SW@@\% per word; Fig.~\ref{fig:how}c).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Is it control, or time? (H1).} Religious words were falling in Isigidimi anyway, so we triangulate over a 2$\times$2 of control and editor with one cell missing. Imvo vs Gqoba's Isigidimi fixes the years (@@SREL@@); Jabavu's two papers fix the editor (@@ECREL@@). Both show the gap: two contrasts with different confounds agree in direction (their $p$-values are uncorrected). Jabavu's Isigidimi predates the years in which the classifier chose religion, so this contrast partly replicates it on held-out text. Were the gap only the trend, Isigidimi would have kept falling after 1884; it did not (@@EDREL@@). A Jabavu-era Imvo as religious as his Isigidimi would have refuted control, and a further fall under Gqoba would have undermined it; neither occurred. Politics instead followed the times, rising within Isigidimi before Imvo existed. Whether Jabavu was right to leave is beyond our data; leaving brought less religion, not clearly more politics. The gap reached beyond editors and is not only a matter of which sections each paper ran: Isigidimi's letter-writers used religious words three times as often as Imvo's, so the papers drew on, or chose, different writers. One wrote that \emph{ilizwi alizanga ukuba silipate, lize ukuba lisipate} (`the Word did not come for us to carry it; it came to carry us', or `rule us'; 1 Nov.\ 1884; our gloss, for speaker review).

\textbf{What the embeddings see (H2).} The framing signal looks like a difference in meaning, not slant: Isigidimi more often uses \emph{umhlaba} for the earth, Imvo for land under law, which alone would move the word in each model. The detection floor makes the null a bound: large framing differences are ruled out, small ones are not.

\textbf{Responsible NLP (H3).} History also reaches today's tools: written isiXhosa took shape in the mission press (cf.\ \citealp{gilmour2007}), and its old spelling penalises only the African-language model, perhaps because Serengeti's vocabulary has twice XLM-R's share of \emph{th}/\emph{kh}/\emph{ph} pieces (@@SERTH@@\% vs @@XLMTH@@\%); a one-model hypothesis. A normalised copy must not replace the source: the mapping is lossy (old \emph{t} may be \emph{t} or \emph{th}). Our results describe two papers, not isiXhosa speakers; cultural readings without speaker review would be extractive \citep{bird2020}.

\textbf{Limitations and conclusion.} Two papers, one window, a missing year, an incomplete 2$\times$2, a heuristic letter split, a domain mismatch, unreviewed glosses (Table S1). The evidence fits control shaping what the papers printed, letters included; effects on political language lie below our detection floor.
