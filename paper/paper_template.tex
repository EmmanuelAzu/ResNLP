\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
Where did missionary control leave its trace in the language of the earliest isiXhosa press: in what the papers covered (salience) or in how they used words (framing)? We compare the missionary \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} with \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded free of missionary control, against frequency-matched random word sets. Salience separates the papers: Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary about 3.3 times as often and Isigidimi religious vocabulary about four times as often. Neither survives correction for 15 tests, but both exceed within-paper era drift and match the archive's own account. Framing evidence is weaker and less specific: embedding spaces differ globally and no single concept is robustly shifted, but political concepts lean towards religious contexts in Isigidimi in all five settings tried, a lean that is partly concept-specific and matched by a neutral control: hypothesis-generating. Historical text costs modern tokenizers 2--12\% more subwords per character; spelling alone raises the cost only for the African-language model (about 2\% per word). The papers differ clearly in what they printed; whether missionary control changed how political language was used lies below what about 300k tokens can reliably detect.}

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Where did missionary control leave its trace in the language of the earliest isiXhosa press?} The IsiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023} holds South Africa's earliest African-language newspapers. \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} (1870--88) was a missionary paper; \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded in 1884 by J.\,T.~Jabavu, was the first African-owned paper, free ``from any missionary control'' \citep[p.~55]{schoots2023}. The archive's authors see Isigidimi's origin ``revealed in the frequency of church and Bible stories'' \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}; earlier studies trace single concepts such as \emph{imfundo} (`education'; \citealp{dewet2021,sigenu2021,qazisa2022}). We add a controlled computational test, using communication research's distinction between \emph{agenda-setting}, what is covered \citep{mccombs1972}, and \emph{framing}, how it is presented \citep{entman1993}. We measure the first as \textbf{salience} (the rate of a concept's vocabulary) and the second as the company a word keeps in embedding space \citep{hamilton2016}. We also ask whether modern tokenizers, which such text must pass through, penalise it \citep{petrov2023,rust2021}. \textbf{H1} The papers differ in the salience of political and religious vocabulary. \textbf{H2} They differ in the framing of political concepts, Isigidimi's lying closer to religious contexts. \textbf{H3} Historical text, and its spelling, raises tokenizer cost.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use the isiXhosa blocks of the archive's human-corrected transcriptions (no spell-checking, so spelling and word division are the concern; \citealp[p.~59]{schoots2023}). The window runs from 1 November 1884 (Imvo's first issue) to 1888; Isigidimi's 1886 editions are missing from the archive \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}. Isigidimi has 286{,}663 tokens, Imvo 1.31M; per year-month we draw Imvo pages to match Isigidimi's token count, ten times.

\textbf{Salience.} Concepts are stems (Table S1): 10 political concepts fixed before testing, four neutral controls, and exploratory religion \emph{tixo}/\emph{kristu}, suggested by an issue-grouped nested-cross-validated classifier (@@CLFACC@@ balanced accuracy). Rate ratios per million tokens carry issue-bootstrap CIs and are ranked against 2000 matched sets (random word types of the same combined frequency); the 15 tests are uncorrected. Held-out windows (Isigidimi 1876--83, Imvo 1889--94) change paper and era together, so they test era sensitivity only.

\textbf{Framing.} We use fastText skip-gram \citep{bojanowski2017}, more stable than word2vec here (@@FTSTAB@@ vs @@W2VSTAB@@). Hyper-parameters were tuned on out-of-window text by seed stability \citep{antoniak2018} until dimension and epochs plateaued (dim 400, window 1, 20 epochs). Window 5 checks sensitivity. A word's \emph{profile shift} is $1-r$ between its cosine profiles over the 500 most frequent shared words in the two spaces \citep{hamilton2016}. The null pools issues from both papers and reassigns them to two token-balanced groups (50 permutations); since real groups differ in frequency and style, each concept is also ranked against 2000 matched sets. A directional test compares similarity to religious and political anchors.

\textbf{Tokenizers.} On modern isiXhosa news \citep{adelani2023} we measure subwords for XLM-R \citep{conneau2020}, Serengeti \citep{adebara2023} and mBERT in (a) modern text, (b) the same text with aspiration marks stripped (\emph{th}$\to$\emph{t} etc.) and (c) historical text. Spelling alone (a to b) is judged per word, since the words are identical; historical text (a to c) per character, since word division differs.

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Agenda more than framing (H1, H2).} The papers differ clearly in what they printed, as missionary control would predict; whether control also changed how contributors wrote, our data cannot show. The salience gap exceeds within-paper drift and matches the archive's account \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}, but is not causal: Isigidimi's religious vocabulary fell from 1876, long before Imvo, and rebounded in 1884--85, which we do not explain; parliamentary vocabulary enters in 1883--84, when the paper appeared fortnightly \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}. Whether \emph{vote} (ratio @@VOTEQ@@, CI spanning 1, drift @@VOTEDRIFT@@$\times$) differed is a question for historians. The gap could come from editors' selection or from writers' choice of paper, which printed text cannot separate; but a gap confined to editorials would show the editors' own voice, whereas one in letters too would show different contributor bases.

\textbf{What the embeddings see (H2).} Words shift globally, not specifically: the most-shifted are function words, verb forms and \emph{xosa}; joining the partly disjunctive forms (\emph{uku ba}) left the gap unchanged (@@GAP1@@, @@GAP2@@). Narrow windows favour grammatical similarity, and widening revealed no hidden profile shift. Since only neighbour overlap persists, one reading, a hypothesis, is that local context (neighbours) differs while overall position barely does; \emph{rulumente} (`government') is glossed ``parliament'' in the archive paper \citep[p.~62]{schoots2023}. The directional lean is therefore exploratory. Framing shifts above the detection floor are ruled out, smaller ones are not.

\textbf{Responsible NLP (H3).} Historical text costs every tokenizer, but spelling alone costs only Serengeti, the African-language model, whose vocabulary has about twice XLM-R's share of entries containing \emph{th}, \emph{kh} or \emph{ph} (@@SERTH@@\% vs @@XLMTH@@\%): a hypothesis from one model, and inflated if MasakhaNEWS was in its pretraining data. Normalising to modern spelling would erase the orthography the archive exists to preserve, and the mapping is not reversible (old \emph{t} may be modern \emph{t} or \emph{th}); who decides that trade-off is an ethical question. The archive preserves a small, mission-educated elite filtered by editors: shifts describe these papers, not isiXhosa speakers, and cultural readings without speaker review would be extractive. Stems and glosses await that review. Limitations: two papers, one window, a missing year, no editor metadata. The papers differ clearly in what they printed; whether missionary control changed how political language was used lies below what about 300k tokens can reliably detect.
