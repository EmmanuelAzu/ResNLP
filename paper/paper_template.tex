\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
From 1884 the missionary \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} and the African-owned \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} ran side by side, both with African editors. We ask whether missionary control shows in \emph{what} they printed (salience) or in \emph{how} they used words (framing). Isigidimi uses religious words about four times as often (exploratory), and Imvo parliamentary words 3.3 times as often (neither survives correction for 15 tests). The religious gap holds in readers' letters too, so it reflects who wrote to each paper, not only the editors. Framing evidence is weak: a religious lean of Isigidimi's political words rests on one word, \emph{umhlaba}, which the papers use in different senses, and a neutral control leans further. Modern tokenizers charge 1.7--12\% more for this text; old spelling alone penalises only the African-language model. Control shaped what the papers printed; whether it shaped how they wrote lies below our test's detection floor.}

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Where did missionary control leave its trace in the language of the earliest isiXhosa press?} The IsiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023} makes this testable. \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} was a missionary paper; \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded in 1884 by Isigidimi's former editor J.\,T.~Jabavu, was the first African-owned isiXhosa paper, free ``from any missionary control'' \citep[p.~55]{schoots2023}. From late 1884 both had African editors, Gqoba and Jabavu \citep[p.~71]{schoots2023b}, so comparing them isolates \emph{control}, not the editor's identity.

Communication research separates \emph{agenda-setting}, what a paper covers \citep{mccombs1972}, from \emph{framing}, how it presents it \citep{entman1993}. We measure the first as \textbf{salience}, how often a topic's words appear, and the second with word embeddings, which place a word by the company it keeps \citep{hamilton2016}. Such studies mostly use modern, high-resource news \citep{card2015,field2018}; for these papers, \citet{schoots2023b} counted root words over time. We add controls that make both kinds of difference testable, a split into readers' letters and the rest to ask \emph{how} control worked, and an audit of what this text costs modern tokenizers \citep{petrov2023,rust2021}. \textbf{H1}: the papers differ in how often they use political and religious words. \textbf{H2}: Isigidimi's political words sit closer to religious contexts. \textbf{H3}: historical text and its spelling raise tokenizer cost.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use the archive's human-corrected transcriptions, which were not spell-checked \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}, from 1 November 1884 to 1888 (Isigidimi's 1886 editions are missing): 286{,}663 tokens of Isigidimi and 1.31M of Imvo.

\textbf{Salience.} Concepts are word stems adapted from \citet{schoots2023b} (Table S1): ten political concepts fixed in advance, four neutral controls, and religion (\emph{tixo}, \emph{kristu}). Religion is exploratory: the archive's authors expect it \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}, but a classifier trained on the same years selected it. Ratios carry issue-bootstrap CIs.

\textbf{Letters.} A letter opens by addressing the editor (\emph{nkosi mhleli \dots\ kaundifakele}, `sir, editor \dots\ please print') and ends with a signature. This rule found @@LETPREC@@ genuine letters in a random hand check (Table S5); letters are @@LETSHI@@\% of Isigidimi and @@LETSHM@@\% of Imvo.

\textbf{Framing.} We train fastText embeddings \citep{bojanowski2017} (window 1, 400 dimensions; more stable than word2vec). A word's \emph{profile shift} is $1-r$ between its similarities to 500 common words in each paper (adapted from \citealp{hamilton2016b}), and a concept's shift is the mean over its forms; \emph{neighbour overlap} compares its ten nearest neighbours \citep{gonen2020}. Chance comes from shuffling issues between papers \citep{dubossarsky2017}. A directional test asks whether political words sit nearer religious or political anchor words (Table S4). To read it, we count religious (heaven, God) and property or political words (title, law) within ten words of \emph{umhlaba}.

\textbf{Tokenizers.} We count subwords for XLM-R \citep{conneau2020}, Serengeti \citep{adebara2023} and mBERT on modern news \citep{adelani2023}, on the same news in old spelling (per word) and on our text (per character, as word division differs).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{How control worked (H1).} Control left a clear trace in what the papers printed, and not only through the editors: readers who wrote to Isigidimi used religious words three times as often as Imvo's, so the papers drew on, or chose, different writers. The gap is not a reaction to Imvo: Isigidimi's religious vocabulary had been falling since 1876, and rose again in 1884--85, around its change of editor and Imvo's arrival (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}a). Parliamentary words entered Isigidimi in 1883--84, late in Jabavu's editorship, when it appeared twice monthly and before the 1884 Cape election \citep{schoots2023b}; these causes cannot be separated (Fig.~S2).

\textbf{What the embeddings see (H2).} The framing signal looks like a difference in meaning, not slant. Isigidimi more often uses \emph{umhlaba} for the earth of creation, Imvo for land under title and law; this alone would place the word differently in each model and could produce the political `lean'. A neutral control behaves similarly, and narrow windows mostly capture grammar. With about 300k tokens per paper, we can rule out large framing differences, not small ones.

\textbf{Responsible NLP (H3).} Old spelling penalises only the model built for African languages, perhaps because Serengeti's vocabulary has twice XLM-R's share of \emph{th}/\emph{kh}/\emph{ph} pieces (@@SERTH@@\% vs @@XLMTH@@\%); this is a one-model hypothesis, and Serengeti may have seen MasakhaNEWS in training. Normalising spelling is no fix: it would erase the orthography the archive preserves, irreversibly (old \emph{t} may be modern \emph{t} or \emph{th}). The archive's proposed machine-learning labelling \citep[p.~61]{schoots2023} would inherit this cost. Our results describe two papers, not isiXhosa speakers; cultural readings without speaker review would be extractive \citep{bird2020}.

\textbf{Limitations and conclusion.} We study two papers in one window, with a missing year, no editor metadata, a heuristic letter split, a modern-news domain mismatch and stems not yet reviewed by a first-language speaker (\emph{rulumente} is `government' here, `parliament' in \citealp[p.~62]{schoots2023}). Within these limits, control shaped what these papers printed, through their writers as well as their editors; whether it shaped how political language was used lies below our test's detection floor.
