\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
Where did missionary control leave its trace in the language of the earliest isiXhosa press: in what the papers covered (salience) or in how they used words (framing)? We compare the missionary \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} with \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded free of missionary control, against frequency-matched random word sets. Salience separates the papers: Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary about 3.3 times as often and Isigidimi religious vocabulary about four times as often. Neither survives correction for 15 tests, but both exceed within-paper era drift and match the archive's own account. Framing evidence is weaker: embedding spaces differ globally, no single concept is robustly shifted, and a lean of political concepts towards religious contexts in Isigidimi is only hypothesis-generating. Historical text costs modern tokenizers 2--12\% more subwords per character; spelling alone raises the cost only for the African-language model (about 2\% per word). The papers differ in what they printed; whether missionary control changed how political language was used is below what 300k tokens can detect.}

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Where did missionary control leave its trace in the language of the earliest isiXhosa press?} The IsiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023} holds South Africa's earliest African-language newspapers. \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} (1870--88) was a missionary paper; \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded in 1884 by Isigidimi's former editor J.\,T.~Jabavu \citep[p.~71]{schoots2023b}, was the first African-owned paper, free ``from any missionary control'' \citep[p.~55]{schoots2023}. The archive's authors see Isigidimi's origin ``revealed in the frequency of church and Bible stories'' \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}. We add a controlled computational test, using communication research's distinction between \emph{agenda-setting}, what is covered \citep{mccombs1972}, and \emph{framing}, how it is presented \citep{entman1993}. We measure the first as \textbf{salience} (rate of a concept's vocabulary), the second as the company a word keeps in embedding space \citep{hamilton2016}. Computational work separating the two exists mainly for modern, high-resource news \citep{card2015,field2018}. For these two papers, \citet{schoots2023b} tracked root-word frequencies over time, showing how editors and elections shaped identity vocabulary (\emph{-ntsundu}, \emph{-mnyama}). We extend that work with frequency-matched baselines for salience, an embedding-based framing test against permuted and matched nulls, and an audit of what historical spelling costs modern tokenizers, which already charge low-resource languages more \citep{petrov2023,rust2021}. Who builds such technology for African languages, and on whose terms, is itself contested \citep{bird2020}. \textbf{H1} The papers differ in the salience of political and religious vocabulary. \textbf{H2} They differ in the framing of political concepts, Isigidimi's lying closer to religious contexts. \textbf{H3} Historical text, and its spelling, raises tokenizer cost.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use the isiXhosa blocks of the archive's human-corrected transcriptions (not spell-checked, so orthography and word division are unstandardised; \citealp[p.~59]{schoots2023}). The window runs from Imvo's first issue (1 November 1884) to 1888; Isigidimi's 1886 editions are missing from the archive \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}. In this window Gqoba edited Isigidimi and Jabavu Imvo \citep[p.~71]{schoots2023b}, so both had African editors; what differs is ownership. Isigidimi has 286{,}663 tokens, Imvo 1.31M; per year-month we draw Imvo pages to match Isigidimi's token count, ten times.

\textbf{Salience.} Concepts are stems (Table S1) matched, following \citet{schoots2023b}, by patterns with exclusions and a check of every matched form: 10 political concepts fixed before testing, four neutral controls, and exploratory religion \emph{tixo}/\emph{kristu} (suggested by a classifier, @@CLFACC@@ balanced accuracy). Rate ratios per million tokens carry issue-bootstrap CIs and are ranked against 2000 matched sets (random word types of the same combined frequency; cf.\ \citealp{monroe2008}); the 15 tests are uncorrected.

\textbf{Framing.} We use fastText skip-gram \citep{bojanowski2017}, more stable than word2vec here (@@FTSTAB@@ vs @@W2VSTAB@@). Hyper-parameters were tuned on out-of-window text by seed stability \citep{antoniak2018} (dim 400, window 1, 20 epochs). A word's \emph{profile shift} is $1-r$ between its cosine profiles over the 500 most frequent shared words in the two spaces \citep{hamilton2016}; windows 2 and 5 test sensitivity. The null pools issues from both papers and reassigns them to two token-balanced groups (50 permutations; cf.\ \citealp{dubossarsky2017}); each concept is also ranked against 2000 matched sets. Neighbour overlap (Jaccard) follows \citet{gonen2020}. A directional test compares similarity to religious and political anchors.

\textbf{Tokenizers.} On modern isiXhosa news \citep{adelani2023} we count subwords for XLM-R \citep{conneau2020}, Serengeti \citep{adebara2023} and mBERT on (a) modern text, (b) the same text with aspiration stripped (\emph{th}$\to$\emph{t}) and (c) historical text; (a to b) per word, (a to c) per character, since word division differs.

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Salience (H1).} Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary @@SPAR@@$\times$ as often (CI @@SPARLO@@--@@SPARHI@@; matched-set $p=@@SPARP@@$) and Isigidimi religious vocabulary four times as often (@@SREL@@$\times$, $p=@@SRELP@@$); neither survives correction for 15 tests ($p<0.003$) and nothing else lies beyond the matched sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, left). Within-paper drift in 1876--94 held-out windows is only @@ERAPR@@$\times$, far below the gaps (government, @@GOVDRIFT@@$\times$, is not interpreted; Table S3).

\textbf{Framing (H2).} Mean profile shift is @@OBS@@ against a permutation null of @@NULL@@, so concepts are judged against matched sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, right). No single concept is robust: parliament alone reaches the @@PARU@@ percentile ($p=@@PARUP@@$), as a neutral control (cattle) does. Pooled parliament and vote shift on neighbour overlap (Jaccard) in all five settings (@@JPVLO@@--@@JPVHI@@ percentile) but on profile only at windows 1--2 (@@W5POOLP@@ at window 5; Table S2). Political concepts lean towards religious anchors in Isigidimi in all five settings (pooled $\Delta$ @@DIRLO@@ to @@DIRHI@@, $p<@@DIRPHI@@$), but without \emph{land} $p$ is @@NOLANDLO@@--@@NOLANDHI@@ and \emph{house} shifts as much. A one-word concept must shift by @@FLOOR1@@ to register, more than the paper-level gap (@@GAP1@@).

\textbf{Tokenizers (H3).} Per word, stripping aspiration raises Serengeti's cost by @@SW@@\% but lowers XLM-R's and mBERT's (@@XW@@\%, @@MW@@\%); per character, historical text costs @@SHI@@\%, @@XHI@@\% and @@MHI@@\% more for Serengeti, XLM-R and mBERT (CIs $\pm@@TCI@@$\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:two2}, right).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Agenda more than framing (H1, H2).} The papers differ clearly in what they printed, as missionary control would predict; whether control also changed how contributors wrote, our data cannot show. The salience gap exceeds drift and matches the archive's account \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}, but is not causal: Isigidimi's religious vocabulary fell from 1876, long before Imvo, and rebounded in 1884--85 (unexplained); parliamentary vocabulary enters Isigidimi in 1883--84, late in Jabavu's 1881--84 editorship \citep[p.~71]{schoots2023b}, when it appeared fortnightly \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023} and before the 1884 election; editor and election effects cannot be separated here. Editors' selection and writers' choice of paper cannot be separated in print, but a gap only in editorials would show the editors' voice, one in letters too different contributor bases.

\textbf{What the embeddings see (H2).} Words shift globally, not specifically (mostly function words and verb forms), and joining partly disjunctive forms (\emph{uku ba}) left the gap unchanged (@@GAP1@@, @@GAP2@@). Narrow windows favour grammatical similarity, and widening revealed no hidden profile shift; since only neighbour overlap persists, local context may differ while overall position barely does; \emph{rulumente} (`government') is glossed ``parliament'' in the archive paper \citep[p.~62]{schoots2023}.

\textbf{Responsible NLP (H3).} Spelling alone costs only Serengeti, the African-language model, whose vocabulary has twice XLM-R's share of entries containing \emph{th}, \emph{kh} or \emph{ph} (@@SERTH@@\% vs @@XLMTH@@\%): a one-model hypothesis, and inflated if MasakhaNEWS was in its pretraining data. Normalising to modern spelling would erase the orthography the archive preserves, and is not reversible (old \emph{t} may be modern \emph{t} or \emph{th}); who decides that trade-off is an ethical question. The archive preserves a small, mission-educated elite filtered by editors: shifts describe these papers, not isiXhosa speakers, and cultural readings without speaker review would be extractive \citep{bird2020}. Stems and glosses await speaker review. Limitations: two papers, one window, a missing year, no editor metadata. Whether missionary control changed how political language was used lies below what 300k tokens can detect.
