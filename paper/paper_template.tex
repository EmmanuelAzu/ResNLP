\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
Isigidimi sama-Xosa was founded by missionaries; Imvo Zabantsundu (1884) was the first African-owned isiXhosa paper. Did it leave a trace in what each paper covered (salience) or in how it used words (framing)? Comparing Isigidimi with ten date-matched Imvo subsamples against frequency-matched random words, we find that salience separates the papers: Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary about 3.3 times as often, Isigidimi religious vocabulary about four times as often, and the religious result replicates in held-out years. Framing is weaker and less specific: embedding spaces differ globally and a pooled parliament-and-vote set is suggestively shifted (@@P1@@--@@P2@@ percentile of matched sets), but no concept survives correction and a directional test is significant in one of four conditions. Historical spelling also costs modern tokenizers 2--12\% more subwords per character, most for an African-language model.}
\begin{quote}\small \textbf{Keywords:} historical isiXhosa; agenda-setting versus framing; tokenizer fairness\end{quote}

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
The IsiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023} digitises South Africa's earliest African-language press. \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} (monthly, 1870--88) was founded by missionaries; \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} (from November 1884) was the first African-owned paper, free ``from any missionary control''. The archive's authors describe Isigidimi's missionary foundation as ``revealed in the frequency of church and Bible stories''; earlier studies trace single concepts (\emph{imfundo}, \emph{impucuko}, \emph{umsebenzi}; \citealp{dewet2021,sigenu2021,qazisa2022}). We add a controlled computational complement built on a distinction from communication research: \emph{agenda-setting}, what is covered \citep{mccombs1972}, versus \emph{framing}, how it is presented \citep{entman1993}. We operationalise them as \textbf{salience} (the rate of a concept's vocabulary) and \textbf{framing} (the company a word keeps in embedding space; \citealp{hamilton2016}), and ask whether modern tokenizers penalise historical spelling \citep{petrov2023,rust2021}. Hypotheses: \textbf{H1} the papers differ in the salience of political and religious vocabulary; \textbf{H2} they differ in the framing of political concepts, Isigidimi's lying closer to religious contexts; \textbf{H3} historical spelling raises tokenizer cost.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use the isiXhosa blocks of the archive's human-corrected transcriptions (no error rate or spell-checking is applied, so spelling and word division are the concern; \citealp{schoots2023}). The window runs from 1 November 1884 (Imvo's first issue) to 1888; Isigidimi's 1886 editions are missing from the archive. Isigidimi has 286{,}663 tokens, Imvo 1.31M. For each year-month with Isigidimi text we draw Imvo pages from that month to match its token count (287k), ten times.

\textbf{Salience.} Concepts are stems (e.g.\ parliament \emph{palamente}, vote \emph{voti}, law \emph{mteto}; 15 in all, with four neutral controls and exploratory religion \emph{tixo}/\emph{kristu}, suggested by an issue-grouped nested-cross-validated classifier: @@CLFACC@@ balanced accuracy once mastheads and datelines are removed). We report rate ratios per million tokens with issue-bootstrap CIs and rank each against 2000 pseudo-concepts (random word-type bags of the same combined frequency). The 15 tests are uncorrected; replication on held-out years (Isigidimi 1876--83 vs Imvo 1889--94) is the safeguard.

\textbf{Framing.} We use fastText skip-gram \citep{bojanowski2017}, more stable than word2vec here (@@FTSTAB@@ vs @@W2VSTAB@@). Hyper-parameters were tuned on out-of-window text by seed stability \citep{antoniak2018} until dimension and epochs plateaued (dim 400, window 1, 20 epochs; 1 is the smallest window). A word's \emph{profile shift} is $1-r$ between its cosine profiles over the 500 most frequent shared words in the two spaces \citep{hamilton2016}. The null pools issues from both papers and reassigns them to two token-balanced groups (50 permutations); because real groups differ in frequency and style but permuted ones do not, each concept is also ranked against 2000 frequency-matched non-concept word sets. A directional test compares similarity to religious and political anchors (top-PMI neighbours of \emph{tixo/kristu} and \emph{palamente/voti} in held-out years). A control joins disjunctively written forms (\emph{uku ba}$\to$\emph{ukuba}).

\textbf{Tokenizers.} On modern isiXhosa news \citep{adelani2023} we measure subwords per 100 characters for XLM-R \citep{conneau2020}, Serengeti \citep{adebara2023} and mBERT in (a) modern text, (b) the same text with aspiration marks (\emph{th, kh, ph, rh, ch, qh, xh}) stripped and (c) historical text. All runs are CPU-only (about three hours, four cores).

\looseness=-1
\textbf{Salience (H1).} Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary @@SPAR@@$\times$ as often (CI @@SPARLO@@--@@SPARHI@@; pseudo-concept $p=@@SPARP@@$) and Isigidimi religious vocabulary four times as often (@@SREL@@$\times$, $p=@@SRELP@@$); nothing else lies beyond the matched null (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, left). \section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Agenda more than framing.} Missionary control is clearest in what the papers covered: Isigidimi's religious vocabulary matches the archive's qualitative account \citep{schoots2023} and replicates out of window. A selection mechanism predicts this: both papers carried correspondents' reports and readers' letters \citep{schoots2023}, so an editor's chief power was choosing what to print, which moves salience (agenda-setting; \citealp{mccombs1972}) without changing how contributors used words (framing; \citealp{entman1993}). Only shifts above the detection floor are excluded, and the pooled parliament--vote shift, consistent across settings but never surviving correction, is suggestive only.

\textbf{Attribution.} Parliamentary vocabulary enters Isigidimi in 1883--84, the end of Jabavu's probable 1881--83 editorship (secondary sources; the archive paper gives no dates), not his arrival in 1881, and coincides with fortnightly publication and the 1884 election; the religious decline began in 1876. An editor effect is not identified.

\textbf{Why framing is hard to see.} Words shift globally but rarely specifically: the most-shifted are function words, verb forms and \emph{xosa}, Isigidimi's spelling of \emph{xhosa}. Joining forms left the gap unchanged, so word division is not the explanation; spelling, register and topic remain open.

\textbf{Responsible NLP.} Historical spelling costs modern tokenizers up to 12\% more subwords per character, most for the African-language model (pretraining data unlisted, overlap unverified). The archive's authors plan to explore machine-learning labelling and named-entity recognition; our result predicts, untested downstream, that such tools will serve 1880s isiXhosa worst, with isiXhosa-speaking researchers and students bearing the errors. Stems, spelling rules and neighbour lists await review by a first-language speaker; \emph{rulumente} is glossed ``parliament'' in the archive paper but treated here as ``government''. Limitations: two papers, one window, a missing year, no editor metadata, a modern-news domain mismatch; voices are mission-educated elites.
