\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
From 1884 the missionary \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} and the African-owned \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} ran side by side. Did missionary control show in \emph{what} they printed (salience) or \emph{how} they used words (framing)? Imvo was not more political in vocabulary: of ten political concepts fixed in advance, only parliament differs (3.3$\times$, uncorrected). Exploratory religion differs most: Isigidimi uses it four times as often, in readers' letters too. Holding the editor constant, Jabavu's religious vocabulary fell to a third when he left Isigidimi, though changes of editor also moved it. Framing evidence is weak: a religious lean of political words rests on \emph{umhlaba}, used in different senses, and a neutral control leans further. Tokenizers charge 1.7--12\% more for this text. Control, alongside the editor, shaped what the papers printed; whether it shaped how they wrote lies below our detection floor.}

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Where did missionary control leave its trace in the language of the earliest isiXhosa press?} In the IsiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023}, \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} was a missionary paper; \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded in 1884 by Isigidimi's former editor J.\,T.~Jabavu, was the first African-owned isiXhosa paper, free ``from any missionary control'' \citep[p.~55]{schoots2023}. From late 1884 both had African editors, Gqoba and Jabavu \citep[p.~71]{schoots2023b}. Comparing the papers contrasts control, but also two editors, places and audiences, which one paper per side cannot separate. So we also hold the editor constant: Jabavu edited Isigidimi in 1881--84, then Imvo.

Communication research separates \emph{agenda-setting}, what a paper covers \citep{mccombs1972}, from \emph{framing}, how it presents it \citep{entman1993}. We measure the first as \textbf{salience}, how often a topic's words appear, and the second with word embeddings \citep{hamilton2016}. Such studies mostly use modern news \citep{card2015,field2018}; \citet{schoots2023b} counted root words in these papers. We add controls that make both testable, the same-editor contrast, and a letters-versus-rest split to ask \emph{how} control worked; finally, we ask whether this history still shapes how machines read the archive \citep{petrov2023,rust2021}. \textbf{H1}: the papers differ in how often they use political and religious words. \textbf{H2}: Isigidimi's political words sit closer to religious contexts. \textbf{H3}: historical text and its spelling raise tokenizer cost.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use the archive's human-corrected transcriptions, not spell-checked \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}, from November 1884 to 1888 (Isigidimi's 1886 editions are missing): 286{,}663 tokens of Isigidimi and 1.31M of Imvo.

\textbf{Salience.} Concepts are word stems adapted from \citet{schoots2023b} (Table S1): ten political concepts fixed in advance, four neutral controls, and religion (\emph{tixo}, \emph{kristu}). Religion is exploratory: the archive's authors expect it \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}, but a classifier trained on the same years chose it (logistic regression on shared words, issue-grouped nested cross-validation, @@CLFACC@@ balanced accuracy). Ratios carry issue-bootstrap CIs; the same-editor contrast uses Isigidimi of January 1881 to October 1884.

\textbf{Letters.} A letter opens by addressing the editor (\emph{nkosi mhleli \dots\ kaundifakele}, `sir, editor \dots\ please print') and ends with a signature; @@LETPREC@@ random detections were genuine letters (Table S5).

\textbf{Framing.} Embedding shift is a weak proxy for framing in Entman's sense: it also reflects sense, grammar and topic. We train fastText embeddings \citep{bojanowski2017} tuned for stability across seeds, since unstable embeddings create false shifts in small corpora \citep{antoniak2018}; this favours a narrow, grammar-heavy window (1), so we also report windows 2 and 5. A word's \emph{profile shift} is $1-r$ between its similarities to 500 common words in each paper \citep{hamilton2016b}; \emph{neighbour overlap} compares its ten nearest neighbours \citep{gonen2020}; chance comes from shuffling issues \citep{dubossarsky2017}. The directional test, the part aimed at framing, asks whether political words sit nearer religious or political anchors (Table S4). To read it, we count religious and legal words near \emph{umhlaba}.

\textbf{Tokenizers.} We count subwords for XLM-R \citep{conneau2020}, Serengeti \citep{adebara2023} and mBERT \citep{devlin2019} on modern news \citep{adelani2023}, on the same news in old spelling (per word) and on our text (per character).



\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{H1: different things, but not more politics.} Of ten political concepts, only parliament differs: Imvo uses it @@SPAR@@ times as often ($p=@@SPARP@@$ against matched sets; Fig.~\ref{fig:agenda}). Law, vote, rights, land and government do not differ beyond chance. Exploratory religion does: Isigidimi uses it @@SRELINV@@ times as often ($p=@@SRELP@@$). Neither survives correction for 15 tests ($p<0.003$). (CIs test whether rates differ; matched sets, whether a concept differs more than random words, which also differ between papers.) The religious gap holds in letters (Imvo/Isigidimi @@LREL@@, CI @@LRELLO@@--@@LRELHI@@) as in the rest (@@RREL@@). With Jabavu as editor of both, Imvo used religious words a third as often as Isigidimi (@@ECREL@@, CI @@ECRELLO@@--@@ECRELHI@@) and parliamentary words @@ECPAR@@ times as often (@@ECPARLO@@--@@ECPARHI@@). Under the same control, Gqoba's Isigidimi used religious words @@EDREL@@ times as often as Jabavu's (@@EDRELLO@@--@@EDRELHI@@). Both span time: Isigidimi's religious rate had already fallen to @@ETREL@@ of its 1876--80 level by 1881--84 (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}a; Table S7).

\textbf{H2: framing differences are weak and not specific.} Word use differs overall (profile shift @@OBS@@ vs @@NULL@@ by chance), but no concept stands out: parliament reaches the @@PARU@@ percentile of matched sets, the control \emph{cattle} the @@CATLO@@--@@CATHI@@ (Fig.~S1). Political words lean towards religious anchors in Isigidimi in all five settings ($\Delta$ @@DIRLO@@ to @@DIRHI@@ $z$-units; Bonferroni-significant in @@NBON@@; Table S2). But without \emph{land} the lean is rarely significant ($p=@@NOLANDLO@@$--$@@NOLANDHI@@$); the control \emph{house} leans further (@@HOUSE2LO@@ to @@HOUSE2HI@@)@@ENDLUSENT@@; and \emph{umhlaba} sits near religious words in @@LSRI@@\% of Isigidimi's uses (Imvo @@LSRM@@\%), near legal or political words in @@LSPI@@\% (Imvo @@LSPM@@\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:how}b). A one-word concept must shift by @@FLOOR1@@ (1.645 matched-set SDs) to be detected, more than the overall gap (@@GAP1@@).

\textbf{H3: historical text costs more.} Per character, our text needs @@SHI@@\%, @@XHI@@\% and @@MHI@@\% more subwords with Serengeti, XLM-R and mBERT; old spelling alone raises only Serengeti's cost (@@SW@@\% per word; Fig.~\ref{fig:how}c).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{How control worked (H1).} What would have refuted control? Jabavu writing alike in both papers. He did not: leaving cut his paper's religious vocabulary to a third. But Gqoba's Isigidimi was more religious than Jabavu's, so the editor matters too; control is one cause among several. Our data cannot judge whether Jabavu was right to leave, but leaving brought less religion and only modestly more politics: Isigidimi already reported on parliament under him. Control also reached beyond editors: Isigidimi's letter-writers used religious words three times as often as Imvo's, so the papers drew on, or chose, different writers. One wrote that \emph{ilizwi alizanga ukuba silipate, lize ukuba lisipate} (`the Word did not come for us to carry it; it came to carry us'; Isigidimi, 1 Nov.\ 1884, our gloss).

\textbf{What the embeddings see (H2).} The framing signal looks like a difference in meaning, not slant: Isigidimi more often uses \emph{umhlaba} for the earth of creation, Imvo for land under title and law, which alone would place the word differently in each model. The detection floor turns this null into a bound: large framing differences are ruled out, small ones are not.

\textbf{Responsible NLP (H3).} The papers' history also reaches today's tools: old spelling penalises only the African-language model, perhaps because Serengeti's vocabulary has twice XLM-R's share of \emph{th}/\emph{kh}/\emph{ph} pieces (@@SERTH@@\% vs @@XLMTH@@\%); this is a one-model hypothesis. Normalising a working copy is reasonable, but it must not replace the source: the mapping is lossy (old \emph{t} may be modern \emph{t} or \emph{th}). Our results describe two papers, not isiXhosa speakers; reading them culturally without speaker review would be extractive \citep{bird2020}.

\textbf{Limitations and conclusion.} Two papers, one window, a missing year, time-bound editor contrasts, a heuristic letter split, a news-domain mismatch, unreviewed stems (\emph{rulumente} is `government' here, `parliament' in \citealp[p.~62]{schoots2023}). So control, alongside the editor, shaped what these papers printed, down to their letter-writers; whether it shaped political language lies below our detection floor.
