\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
Where did missionary control leave its trace in the earliest isiXhosa press: in what papers covered (salience) or how they used words (framing)? We compare the missionary \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} with \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded free of missionary control. Salience separates the papers: Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary 3.3 times as often; Isigidimi religious vocabulary four times as often (exploratory). Neither survives correction for 15 tests; the religious gap exceeds era drift and matches the archive's account, the parliamentary gap exceeds drift on its point estimate only. Framing evidence is weaker: embedding spaces differ globally and no single concept is robustly shifted; a lean of political words towards religious contexts in Isigidimi survives correction in three of five settings, but in at most one without \emph{land}, and a neutral control leans further. Historical text costs modern tokenizers 1.7--12\% more subwords per character; spelling alone raises it only for the African-language model. The papers differ in what they printed; whether missionary control changed how political language was used is below our test's detection floor.}

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Where did missionary control leave its trace in the language of the earliest isiXhosa press?} The IsiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023} holds South Africa's earliest African-language newspapers. \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} was a missionary paper; \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded in 1884 by Isigidimi's former editor J.\,T.~Jabavu \citep[p.~71]{schoots2023b}, was the first African-owned paper, free ``from any missionary control'' \citep[p.~55]{schoots2023}. The archive's authors see Isigidimi's origin ``revealed in the frequency of church and Bible stories'' \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}. We add a controlled test, using the communication-research distinction between \emph{agenda-setting}, what is covered \citep{mccombs1972}, and \emph{framing}, how it is presented \citep{entman1993}. We measure the first as \textbf{salience} (a concept's vocabulary rate), the second as the company a word keeps in embedding space \citep{hamilton2016}. Computational work separating them is mainly on modern, high-resource news \citep{card2015,field2018}. For these papers, \citet{schoots2023b} tracked root-word frequencies, showing how editors and elections shaped identity vocabulary. We add frequency-matched baselines for salience, an embedding-based framing test against permuted and matched nulls, and an audit of what historical spelling costs modern tokenizers, which already charge low-resource languages more \citep{petrov2023,rust2021}. \textbf{H1} Salience of political and religious vocabulary differs. \textbf{H2} Framing of political concepts differs, Isigidimi's lying closer to religious contexts. \textbf{H3} Historical text and its spelling raise tokenizer cost.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use the isiXhosa blocks of the archive's human-corrected transcriptions (no automated spell-checking; \citealp[p.~59]{schoots2023}); word division varies (Table S1). The window runs from Imvo's first issue (1 November 1884) to 1888; Isigidimi's 1886 editions are missing \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023}. Gqoba edited Isigidimi and Jabavu Imvo in this window \citep[p.~71]{schoots2023b}, so both had African editors; ownership differs. Isigidimi has 286{,}663 tokens, Imvo 1.31M; per month we draw Imvo pages matching Isigidimi's size, ten times.

\textbf{Salience.} Concepts are stems (Table S1) adapting \citet{schoots2023b} and inspecting the most frequent matched forms for false positives: 10 political concepts fixed before testing, four neutral controls, and exploratory religion (\emph{tixo}, \emph{kristu}): anticipated by the archive \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023} but selected by an issue-grouped nested-CV classifier trained on the test window (0.83 balanced accuracy). Rate ratios carry issue-bootstrap CIs and are ranked against 2000 matched sets (random word types of equal combined frequency, an alternative to log-odds ratios, \citealp{monroe2008}); the 15 tests are uncorrected.

\textbf{Framing.} We use fastText skip-gram \citep{bojanowski2017}, more stable than word2vec here (seed stability, top-10 overlap: 0.83 vs 0.64). Hyper-parameters (dim 400, window 1, 20 epochs) were tuned for seed stability \citep{antoniak2018} on out-of-window text. A word's \emph{profile shift} is $1-r$ between its cosine profiles over the 500 most frequent shared words in the two spaces (adapted from \citealp{hamilton2016b}); a concept's shift is the unweighted mean of its forms' shifts. The null pools issues from both papers and reassigns them to two token-balanced groups (50 permutations; cf.\ \citealp{dubossarsky2017}); concepts are also ranked against 2000 matched sets. Neighbour overlap (Jaccard) follows \citet{gonen2020}; a directional test compares similarity to religious and political anchors (Table S4).

\textbf{Tokenizers.} On modern isiXhosa news \citep{adelani2023} we count subwords for XLM-R \citep{conneau2020}, Serengeti \citep{adebara2023} and mBERT for (a) modern text, (b) the same with aspiration stripped and (c) historical text; (a to b) per word, (a to c) per character, since historical word division differs.

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Salience (H1).} Imvo uses parliamentary vocabulary 3.3$\times$ as often ($p=0.018$) and Isigidimi religious vocabulary four times as often (0.24$\times$, $p=0.020$); neither survives correction for 15 tests ($p<0.003$) and nothing else lies beyond the matched sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, left). Across held-out eras (Isigidimi 1876--83, Imvo 1889--94), Isigidimi's parliamentary rate moves 1.8$\times$ (CI to 5.0, overlapping the gap); \emph{vote} rises 8.5$\times$ within Isigidimi and government falls in both papers, so we interpret neither (Table S3).

\textbf{Framing (H2).} Mean profile shift (0.348) exceeds the permutation null (0.230): a global gap of 0.118, so we test concepts against matched sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:two}, right). No single concept is robust: parliament reaches the 96th percentile ($p=0.045$), while a neutral control (cattle) reaches the 90th--99th across settings. Pooled parliament and vote exceed matched sets on neighbour overlap (Jaccard; 94th--98th percentile, $p<0.05$ in three of five settings) but less consistently on profile (86th--99th at windows 1--2, 62nd at window 5; $p<0.05$ in one; Table S2). Political concepts lean towards religious anchors in Isigidimi in all five settings (pooled $\Delta$ +0.44 to +0.76 in $z$-score units; $p\leq0.016$), surviving Bonferroni over five settings ($p<0.01$) in three, but without \emph{land} $p$ is 0.010--0.256 and the control \emph{house} leans further ($\Delta$ +0.84 to +1.51, $p<0.001$ in four; Table S2). A one-word concept must shift by 0.15 (1.645 matched-set SDs, normal approximation), above the global gap.

\textbf{Tokenizers (H3).} Per word, stripping aspiration raises Serengeti's cost by 2.2\%, lowers XLM-R's and mBERT's ($-$2.2\%, $-$3.4\%); per character, historical text costs 12.4\%, 4.1\% and 1.7\% more for Serengeti, XLM-R and mBERT (CIs $\pm0.5$\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:two2}c).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Agenda more than framing (H1, H2).} The papers differ clearly in what they printed, as missionary control would predict; whether it changed how contributors wrote, we cannot show. The religious gap matches the archive's account \citep[p.~57]{schoots2023}; the parliamentary gap has no such corroboration. Neither is causal: Isigidimi's religious vocabulary fell from 1876, long before Imvo, and rebounded in 1884--85, coinciding with Gqoba's editorship and Imvo's competition; parliamentary vocabulary enters Isigidimi in 1883--84, late in Jabavu's 1881--84 editorship \citep[p.~71]{schoots2023b}, when it appeared twice monthly \citep[p.~59]{schoots2023} and before the 1884 Cape election \citep{schoots2023b}; editor and election effects cannot be separated. A gap only in editorials would show the editors' voice; one in letters too, different contributor bases (editors' selection or writers' choice, inseparable in print).

\textbf{What the embeddings see (H2).} The lean is not specific to political words: the control \emph{house} leans further, possibly via the anchor \emph{endlu} (Table S4). Narrow windows favour grammatical similarity; local context may differ, overall position barely. The lean also depends on \emph{land} (\emph{umhlaba}, also `earth', `world') and perhaps mixed-register anchors, for a speaker to check.

\textbf{Responsible NLP (H3).} Spelling alone costs only Serengeti, whose vocabulary has twice XLM-R's share of \emph{th}/\emph{kh}/\emph{ph} entries (1.9\% vs 0.9\%): a one-model hypothesis (MasakhaNEWS overlap possible). Normalising spelling would erase the orthography the archive preserves, irreversibly (old \emph{t} may be modern \emph{t} or \emph{th}). Shifts describe these papers, not isiXhosa speakers; cultural readings without speaker review would be extractive \citep{bird2020}. The archive's proposed machine-learning labelling \citep[p.~61]{schoots2023}, and any model-based analysis of it, would inherit both the subword cost and the extractive risk. Limitations: two papers, one window, a missing year, no editor metadata, era-confounded held-out windows, a modern-news domain mismatch, unreviewed stems and glosses (\emph{rulumente}: `government' here, `parliament' in \citealp[p.~62]{schoots2023}). Whether missionary control changed political language lies below our test's detection floor.
